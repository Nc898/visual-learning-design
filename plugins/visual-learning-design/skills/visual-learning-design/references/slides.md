# Slide decks and slideshows

First decide which of two situations you're in. They pull the principles in opposite directions.

| | **Presented** (live talk, recorded lecture, narrated deck) | **Read** (sent or self-paced, no speaker) |
|---|---|---|
| Pacing | System-paced | Learner-paced |
| Words | The *speaker* carries the explanation. Slides hold graphics and short phrases (modality, redundancy) | Printed words carry it, built into the graphics (spatial contiguity) |
| Danger | Reading paragraphs off the slide | Speaker slides sent out unchanged, so nothing explains the graphics |

When one deck must serve both, make the **slides for the presented case** and put the explanation in **speaker notes or a separate handout**. A slide that tries to be a document fails as both.

## The core slide pattern: assertion-evidence

- **Headline: a full-sentence assertion** of the slide's one takeaway, in **about 12 words or fewer**, so it fits on two lines at presentation size ("Light frees electrons to cross the junction", 7 words). Cut clauses that explain *why*, because that's what your voice and the visual are for. "A vaccine gives your body a safe first meeting with the antigen, so memory cells form without you getting sick" (20 words) becomes "A vaccine is a safe first meeting with the antigen" (10 words). This is signaling: the learner knows what to look for.
- **Body: visual evidence** for that assertion: a diagram, a chart, a photo that shows the thing, or an annotated equation. No bullet list.
- Research on assertion-evidence slides (Alley, Garner, and colleagues) reports better comprehension and fewer misconceptions than topic-plus-bullets slides, especially for complex concepts.
- Use bullets only for content that really is a list (the three conditions, the steps), and keep them short and parallel.

## Applying the principles slide by slide

- **Pre-training.** Early on, show a slide with the key parts labeled on one diagram ("Here's what's in a solar cell"). Reuse that same diagram, with the same layout and colors, through the rest of the deck so the learner's mental model stays anchored.
- **Segmenting.** One idea per slide. Build complex diagrams step by step with animation builds, each build matching the sentence you're saying (temporal contiguity). For a recorded or self-paced deck, the learner's "next" click is the segment control.
- **Signaling.** Use the assertion headlines, an agenda or outline slide that recurs at section breaks with the current section highlighted, arrows and highlights on the diagram, and pointer words in speech ("the second reason…"). Dim what isn't being discussed.
- **Spatial contiguity.** Put labels on the diagram, not in a legend. Write call-out text next to the arrow it explains. Avoid a caption block across the bottom of the slide.
- **Coherence.** Cut:
  - logos, footers, and slide numbers repeated on every slide (one title or end slide carries the brand)
  - decorative templates with textured backgrounds
  - stock photos and clip art
  - flashy transitions
  - "fun" tangents
  - any slide that doesn't serve the goal

  Mayer's own redesign example removes a tangential celebrity video clip and a slogan logo from a solar-cell lecture.
- **Redundancy and modality (presented case).** Show a few words per slide: labels, key terms, numbers. Say the explanation out loud. When the learner must *see* a technical term or formula, put that on screen too.
- **Word budget (presented case): about 25 words of on-screen text per slide, *counting the headline*.** Count before you finish each slide. When a slide goes over, the fix depends on why:
  - **A list of types or examples** (vaccine types, four kinds of rock): put the short names on the visual and the definitions in the speaker notes, or split it into one slide per item.
  - **A long headline:** shorten it (see the assertion-evidence pattern above).
  - **Activity text the students must read** (a scenario or question for an exercise): this is the one exception, because they'll refer to it while working. Keep it on the slide and say so in the rationale.

## The presenter's half

The principles apply to the person as well as to the pixels:

- **Embodiment.** Stand near the screen. Point at or physically gesture to the part you're talking about. Look at the audience. When you can, **draw or annotate live**: building the diagram while explaining beats gesturing at a finished one.
- **Temporal contiguity.** Choreograph speech to the builds: name the thing as it appears, and point while you explain it.
- **Voice and personalization.** Warm, confident, conversational: "Let's see what happens when…" rather than "This presentation describes…". Use vocal emphasis on key terms.
- **Generative activity.** Every 10–15 minutes, ask for a prediction ("What happens if we double the voltage? Commit to a guess."), a one-sentence summary of the last section, a quick sketch, a think-pair-share, or a retrieval question. Show the answer *after* they commit.
- **Image.** In recorded lectures, keep the speaker's camera small or cut away to the content most of the time. Bring it back for intros and transitions, or when the speaker's gestures carry meaning.

## Read (self-paced) decks

- Treat the deck as an illustrated document. Use full-sentence assertion headlines plus graphics with *integrated* explanatory text placed next to the parts.
- Add a navigation cue (section tracker) and a short summary slide per section.
- Add an occasional "check yourself" slide with the answer on the following slide, to prompt retrieval.

## Visual design basics that serve learning

- **Big type and high contrast** (WCAG 4.5:1 for text). Make text readable at the back of the room or on a phone. As a rough floor, keep body text at 24pt or more for presented slides.
- **One focal point per slide.** Make the essential element the largest and highest-contrast thing on it.
- **Consistent color coding** of concepts across the whole deck.
- **Charts:** direct labels, an annotated takeaway, a minimal number of gridlines, and a title that states the claim.

## Checklist (slide-specific)

- [ ] Every content slide has a one-sentence assertion headline of about 12 words or fewer.
- [ ] Presented slides stay within about 25 on-screen words each, headline included. Activity text students must read is the only exception.
- [ ] The slide body is visual evidence, not a paragraph or a bullet list (unless the content really is a list).
- [ ] There is an early labeled pre-training slide, and its diagram is reused.
- [ ] Builds match the narration line by line.
- [ ] No repeated logos or decorative templates, and no irrelevant images or video clips.
- [ ] Labels sit on the diagram, and no legends are needed.
- [ ] There is a generative prompt at least once per section.
- [ ] Speaker notes or a handout carry the full explanation for readers.
