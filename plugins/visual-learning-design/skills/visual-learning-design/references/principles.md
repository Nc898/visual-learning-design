# Principles in depth

Read this when a boundary condition is in play, when two principles pull against each other, or when you need to explain a choice to the user. Each entry gives the principle's **statement**, its **mechanism** (why it works, so you can reason about cases the research didn't test), the **evidence**, how to **apply** it, when it **weakens or reverses**, and common **misreadings**.

Effect sizes are Mayer's (2021) median Cohen's *d* unless noted. Most studies used short (under 30 minute), system-paced lessons on how a system works (lightning, brakes, pumps, lungs), taught to novice college students and measured with transfer tests. Effects are generally smaller in long, authentic courses, but they point the same way. For example, a redesigned medical-school slide lecture produced *d* = 0.76 on immediate transfer and *d* = 1.17 on delayed transfer (Issa et al., 2013).

## Foundation

### Multimedia principle
**Statement.** People learn more deeply from words and pictures than from words alone. *d* = 1.35 (13 tests).
**Mechanism.** With both a verbal and a pictorial model in mind, the learner can build connections between them. Connecting the two models is the integration step, and it is where understanding forms.
**Apply.** Every essential relation in step 2 should have a visual counterpart. Choose a graphic by its function (representational, organizational, relational, transformational, interpretive) and check that it *explains*, not that it merely *appears*.
**Weakens.** For experts, who can build the image themselves. When the graphic is decorative, which turns it into a seductive detail.
**Misreading.** Reading it as "more media is better." The principle concerns *relevant* graphics paired with words. A photo of smiling students beside a paragraph about photosynthesis fails it.

## Reduce extraneous processing

### Coherence principle
**Statement.** People learn more deeply when extraneous material is excluded rather than included. *d* = 0.86 (18 of 19).
**Mechanism.** Extraneous material uses up capacity and pulls selection toward the wrong things. Seductive details (interesting but irrelevant) are the worst offenders, because they also prime the wrong schema for organizing the lesson.
**Evidence highlights.** Interesting facts about viruses hurt learning about how a cold works. Video clips of lightning strikes hurt a lightning-formation lesson. Background music and sound effects hurt narrated animations (Moreno & Mayer, 2000). A 2020 meta-analysis (Sundararajan & Adesope) confirms a reliable seductive-details penalty, largest in short lessons and for novices.
**Apply.** Remove:
- background music and ambient sound
- stock photos and decorative illustration
- fun-fact sidebars and tangential anecdotes
- textured or animated backgrounds
- logos and footers repeated on every screen
- gratuitous transitions
- extra detail beyond the goal: history, edge cases, numbers nobody will use

Keep a story or example only when it *is* the explanation, meaning it carries the causal structure.
**Weakens.** In learner-paced material, where the learner can skip it. For learners with high working memory. When the extra material is neutral rather than highly interesting.
**Misreading.** Reading it as "make it boring" or "remove all examples." Concrete, relevant examples are essential content. Interest should come from relevance and social presence.

### Signaling principle
**Statement.** People learn more deeply when cues highlight the organization of the essential material. *d* = 0.70 (26 of 28). Verbal cues: 0.69. Visual cues: 0.71.
**Mechanism.** Cues guide *selection* (where to look or listen) and *organization* (how the pieces fit).
**Apply.**
- *Verbal cues:* an initial outline ("three things happen"), headings, pointer words ("first… second… as a result…"), vocal stress on key terms, a sentence headline that states the takeaway.
- *Visual cues:* arrows, spotlighting or dimming the rest, color highlights, zoom, a cursor or hand pointing, numbered call-outs that match the narration's order.
- *Linked cues:* one color for one concept, used in the text, the diagram, and the code. Hovering a term highlights its part of the graphic.
**Weakens.** For experts, simple material, and heavy-handed use. Cues compete with each other. If five things are bold, nothing is.
**Misreading.** Decorating with emphasis. A cue must point at the essential element or the structure, and nowhere else.

### Redundancy principle
**Statement.** People do not learn more deeply when printed text that duplicates the narration is added to narrated graphics. *d* = 0.10 (8 of 12): a small effect.
**Mechanism.** The redundant text pulls the eyes away from the graphic, and the learner spends effort reconciling two verbal streams.
**Apply.** In narrated, system-paced media, put only brief labels, key terms, numbers, and step names on screen. Never put the full script there. Offer captions as a closed, toggleable track.
**Weakens or reverses.**
- learner-paced or slow lessons
- short or reworded on-screen text
- learners working in a second language
- no graphics to compete with
- technical terms the learner must see spelled out

**Misreading.** "Never show text with audio" and "turn off captions." Both are wrong: brief key-term text helps, and captions are an access requirement. This principle concerns *default, burned-in, verbatim* on-screen text.

### Spatial contiguity principle
**Statement.** People learn more deeply when printed words sit near the corresponding part of the graphic rather than far from it. *d* = 0.82 (9 of 9). Broader meta-analyses agree (Ginns, 2006; Schroeder & Cenkci, 2018).
**Mechanism.** Removes *split attention*: the learner doesn't have to hold one part in mind while searching for its partner.
**Apply.**
- Label diagrams directly with leader lines instead of a legend or key.
- Annotate charts at the data point.
- Put feedback next to the answer it concerns.
- Put instructions on the same screen as the task.
- Put a code explanation beside the line it explains.
- Keep a figure on the same page or scroll position as the text that discusses it.
- On mobile, make sure stacking doesn't separate a label from its graphic.

**Weakens.** For experts, simple lessons, and redundant text. It is strongest for novices, complex lessons, and interactive formats.

### Temporal contiguity principle
**Statement.** People learn more deeply when the narration and the corresponding graphics are presented *simultaneously* rather than one after the other. *d* = 1.31 (8 of 8).
**Mechanism.** Both representations have to be in working memory at once to be connected.
**Apply.**
- Script each line of narration against the exact visual it describes (a two-column script).
- Trigger builds and animations on the word that names them.
- In live talks, point at the thing while you say it.
- In interactive pieces, update the visual as each explanatory step appears (scrollytelling sync).

**Weakens.** For very short segments, and when the learner controls the pace.

## Manage essential processing

### Segmenting principle
**Statement.** People learn more deeply when a lesson comes in learner-paced segments rather than as one continuous unit. *d* = 0.67 (7 of 7).
**Mechanism.** The learner can finish processing one step (organize and integrate it) before the next arrives.
**Evidence highlight.** A narrated lightning animation was split into 16 segments of about 10 seconds, each followed by a "continue" button.
**Apply.**
- Cut at *meaningful* boundaries: one step, one idea, one cause leading to one effect.
- Give an explicit control to advance (continue, next, chapters, step buttons).
- For video, keep each video short (6 minutes or less is a good engagement target) and add chapter markers.

**Weakens.** For simple material or material that is already slow-paced.
**Misreading.** Chopping content into arbitrary fixed-length pieces, or segments that end mid-idea.

### Pre-training principle
**Statement.** People learn more deeply when they already know the names and characteristics of the main concepts. *d* = 0.78 (10 of 10).
**Mechanism.** Splits learning into two stages. First the learner builds a model of each component (what it is, what states it can be in). Then they learn the causal model of how the components interact, without having to process both at once.
**Apply.**
- A labeled "meet the parts" screen or slide.
- A short glossary interaction.
- A quick tour of the tool's interface before the task.
- A 30-second "here are the players" opening in a video.
- In software, make sure learners can operate the tool before they learn the concept through it.

**Weakens.** For high prior knowledge, simple material, or learner-paced lessons.

### Modality principle
**Statement.** People learn more deeply from graphics with *spoken* words than from graphics with *printed* words. *d* = 1.00 (18 of 19). It is the most-studied principle.
**Mechanism.** Moves the verbal load off the overloaded visual channel and onto the auditory one.
**Apply.** In fast or system-paced pieces with graphics (video, animation, presented slides), narration carries the explanation.
**Weakens or reverses.** Print can be better when:
- words are technical, formulas, or code
- the learner is working in a second language
- the lesson is learner-paced
- the learner needs to re-read or refer back
- segments are long, because speech is transient and can't be held in working memory (the transient-information effect)

**Misreading.** "Audio always beats text." For a self-paced web lesson, well-integrated printed text is often the right call. Modality is about *pacing and channel load*.

## Foster generative processing

Most of these use **social cues**. When the learner feels in a conversation with a person (*social presence*), they try harder to understand.

### Personalization principle
**Statement.** People learn more deeply when the words are conversational rather than formal. *d* = 1.00 (13 of 15). Confirmed by meta-analysis (Ginns, Martin & Marsh, 2013).
**Apply.** Use first and second person ("your heart pumps…", "let's see what happens"), plain everyday words, and a polite tone. Talk to one learner.
**Weakens.** For experienced learners and long lessons. It can backfire when it turns into slang or false familiarity, or when it is rude. Politeness itself matters.

### Voice principle
**Statement.** People learn more deeply when narration uses an appealing human voice rather than a machine voice. *d* = 0.74 (6 of 7).
**Current state.** The original studies used 2000s-era TTS. Newer research (Craig & Schroeder, 2017, 2018) found that high-quality modern TTS performs about as well as human recordings. The real rule is to *avoid voices that sound mechanical, flat, or unfriendly*. Warmth, enthusiasm, natural prosody, and clear stress on key terms matter more than whether a human or a machine produced the audio.
**Apply.** Use a human narrator or a top-tier neural TTS voice. Choose a natural pace and slow down for dense or novice material. Use vocal emphasis as a signaling cue.

### Embodiment principle
**Statement.** People learn more deeply when on-screen instructors or agents show human-like gesture, movement, eye contact, and facial expression. *d* = 0.58 (16 of 17).
**Evidence highlights.**
- An animated agent that gestured and gazed beat a static agent.
- Watching the instructor *draw* the diagram while explaining beat the instructor gesturing at an already-finished diagram (Fiorella & Mayer, 2016).
- A *first-person* (over-the-shoulder) demo camera beat a third-person camera for an assembly task (Fiorella et al., 2017).
- Pointing gestures help (Davis, 2018).

**Apply.** If a person or agent is on screen, have them point at, look at, and build the content. Prefer an instructor who draws or annotates live to a pre-built slide with a talking head.
**Weakens.** When it comes with negative social cues such as a robotic voice.

### Image principle
**Statement.** People do *not necessarily* learn more deeply when the speaker's image is on screen. *d* = 0.19 (4 of 7, with 3 negative).
**Mechanism.** A static face competes for visual attention without adding information. Schroeder and Adesope (2014) summarized it this way: "voice may be critical, whereas the image of the agent is not."
**Apply.** Drop a permanent picture-in-picture talking head or static avatar unless it is embodied (see embodiment). A short face-to-camera intro or outro for rapport is fine. Engagement data (Guo et al., 2014) suggest that cutting between the instructor and the content holds attention better than slides alone.

### Immersion principle
**Statement.** People do not necessarily learn better in immersive 3D/VR than in a corresponding 2D desktop presentation.
**Evidence highlights.**
- Immersive VR raised presence and enjoyment but *lowered* learning and raised cognitive load in a science-lab simulation (Makransky, Terkildsen & Mayer, 2019).
- Adding a generative activity, such as summarizing after each VR segment, recovered learning (Parong & Mayer, 2018).

**Apply.** Choose VR or 3D for tasks that are truly spatial, embodied, or situational. Apply every extraneous-load principle with extra force, because immersive environments are full of seductive detail. Build in generative pauses.

### Generative activity principle
**Statement.** People learn more deeply when they are *prompted and guided* to carry out generative learning activities during learning. *d* = 0.71 (37 of 44).
**Activities** (Fiorella & Mayer, 2015, with median *d* and number of tests):

| Activity | *d* (tests) | Prompt example |
|---|---|---|
| Summarizing | 0.50 (30) | "In one sentence, what just caused the pressure drop?" |
| Mapping | 0.62 (39) | Drag the parts into a concept map or flow diagram |
| Drawing | 0.40 (28) | "Sketch where the electrons go" |
| Imagining | 0.65 (22) | "Picture the valve closing. What happens upstream?" |
| Self-testing | 0.57 (76) | Retrieval questions after each segment |
| Self-explaining | 0.61 (54) | "Why did step 3 have to come before step 4?" |
| Teaching | 0.77 (19) | "Explain this to a classmate who missed it" |
| Enacting | 0.51 (49) | Manipulate the objects or controls to act out the process |

**Apply.** Place a prompt at the end of each segment, ask for a prediction *before* a reveal, and give explanatory feedback after each response.
**Weakens.** When learners get no guidance or training in the activity, or when the activity is too demanding for novices. Scaffold it with partial maps, sentence starters, or a drawing template.

## Related cognitive-load effects

These come from cognitive load theory (Sweller, Kalyuga, Paas) and from Clark and Mayer's *e-Learning and the Science of Instruction*. They fill gaps that the multimedia principles leave, especially in interactive and practice-heavy media.

### Expertise reversal
Support that helps novices (integrated explanations, worked examples, pre-training, heavy signaling) becomes redundant for more knowledgeable learners and can *hurt* them, because they must process material they no longer need (Kalyuga, 2007). **Design response:** assess or ask about prior knowledge. Let experts skip or collapse supports. Fade guidance as performance improves; adaptive fading beats fixed fading (Salden et al., 2010).

### Worked examples, then fading
Novices learn problem-solving faster from studying worked examples than from solving equivalent problems on their own. **Fade**: start with a full example, then present examples with steps missing for the learner to complete, then independent problems. Add **self-explanation prompts** to each worked step. Use examples that vary on the surface but share the same underlying structure, so learners abstract the principle.

### Transient information
Spoken words and animation vanish as they play. When a segment is long or complex, the learner can't hold it in working memory, so the benefits of narration and animation shrink or reverse. **Design response:** keep transient segments short, add pause, replay, and step controls, leave persistent key-frame summaries on screen, and switch to static or printed form for complex material the learner must refer back to.

### Animation vs. static graphics
On average, animation has a modest advantage over static pictures. Estimates run from *d* = 0.37 (Höffler & Leutner, 2007) down to *g* = 0.23 in a later, larger analysis (Berney & Bétrancourt, 2016). The advantage is largest for *procedural-motor* skills, for realistic video, and when the specific changes themselves must be learned. Tversky et al. (2002) give two design tests:
- **Congruence**: the motion in the graphic should match the change in the concept.
- **Apprehension**: the learner must be able to perceive the motion accurately. Too fast, too much, or too smooth fails this test.

Interactivity (pause, step, scrub, replay) helps an animation pass both.

### Multiple representations
Pairing representations (a diagram with an equation, a graph with a table) helps only when the learner is shown how they map onto each other. Signal the correspondences with shared color, linked highlighting, or explicit "this bar is this term" labeling. Unlinked representations add extraneous load.

### Practice and feedback
Practice that resembles the target performance, spaced over time, with **explanatory** feedback beats corrective-only feedback ("Not quite. Resistance went *up*, so current must go down, because…" rather than "Incorrect"). Place feedback beside the response (spatial contiguity). Keep the feedback about the task and the process, not about ego: no praise-heavy messages and no leaderboards.

### Learner control
More learner control (pacing, navigation, sequence) helps learners with high prior knowledge and good metacognition. Novices often choose poorly. They skip practice and overrate their understanding. **Default:** give everyone pacing control (play, pause, continue). Guide the sequence for novices with a recommended path and advice on what to do next. Open up navigation for experts.

## Note on principle lists

Popular summaries, including the Digital Learning Institute article this skill was seeded from, list Mayer's *classic 12*: multimedia, coherence, signaling, redundancy, spatial contiguity, temporal contiguity, segmenting, pre-training, modality, personalization, voice, and image. Mayer's 3rd edition (2021) organizes 15 design principles by load and adds **embodiment**, **immersion**, and **generative activity**. The *Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning* (3rd ed., 2022) expands the list to about 30, including expertise reversal and multiple representations. This skill uses the 15 plus the cognitive-load effects above. See [sources.md](sources.md).
